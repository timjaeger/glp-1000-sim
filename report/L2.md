## The world of 2036

### The world in 600 words

At the end of 2036 a little over one American adult in three takes an incretin drug, close to ninety-eight million people, and almost half have tried one. Nobody finds this interesting anymore. What gets attention is the person who isn't on one. Dentists' intake forms ask for a reason. Dating profiles list 'off by choice' the way they once listed 'vegan'. In a Pew survey this spring, three in five adults said a person not taking one usually had a reason worth asking about.

Prices fell steadily for years and then stopped. In March 2035 a Philadelphia jury returned the first pancreas verdict against Novo, and a Delaware court kept generic semaglutide off the market. After that neither Novo, Lilly nor Amgen cut a single price. The Wegovy pill still starts at $149 on TrumpRx, MariTide is still $219, and Lilly's 12 mg retatrutide, the dose that comes closest to surgery, is still $299. Lilly cannot make enough of it. When the FDA put it on the shortage list, compounding pharmacies were legally allowed to sell copies, and telehealth sites now sell them for $129 to $199. The import trade grew at the same time. India's parcel rules sent shipments through Dubai and Mexico instead of stopping them, and Chinese research-peptide vials now include amycretin and cagrilintide copies as well as 'reta'. More than twenty-two million Americans use something outside the licensed system.

Employers did start paying, with conditions attached. Employer coverage rose from 29 to 37 percent in two years, and nearly all of the increase is what UnitedHealthcare and Cigna call earned coverage. The plan pays for six months. After that it keeps paying only if the pharmacy and the connected scale show the worker took every dose and lost enough weight. About one enrollee in five misses a checkpoint. They lose coverage, often pay a higher premium under the 2027 rule that still allows it, and roughly half of them buy vials. Insurers book the drop-off as savings, and gyms sell checkpoint-prep packages.

Children are now part of the market. In December 2035 the FDA approved weekly Wegovy for children aged six to eleven. The label requires growth monitoring, because children who start early still grow about seven millimetres a year more slowly and nobody yet knows their adult height. In thirty-nine states pediatricians now prescribe it, and in Charlotte a nine-year-old's HbA1c sits in the family portal next to a parent's DXA scan. In the eleven states that banned under-twelve prescribing in 2034, the bans stand, because no legislature anywhere acted on incretins in 2035 or 2036. Some Memphis parents drive to clinics in Southaven, Mississippi. Families on Medicaid mostly do not make the trip.

The quiet is being sold as a product. The 2034 consent orders bind four platforms, and newer ones are not covered. Quiet, the largest of them, has 1.8 million subscribers and advertises on podcasts with the line 'freedom from wanting'. Its ads show couples who have stopped fighting. Couples' therapists see couples who stopped fighting and stopped most other things too. The rate of flattening at low doses has still not been measured. Sportsbooks run ads aimed at young men who are off the drug. Slow television is a mainstream category. Freight hubs outside Columbus still promote monitored-track workers to shift lead at twice the usual rate, and the lawsuit over that practice goes to trial next year.

In the 2036 campaign some candidates mention their own prescriptions in passing and nobody asks a follow-up question. Spirits volumes are down a fifth from 2025 and snack volumes by about a sixth. Lunchbox snack makers can now see the state bans in their sales data. Nothing has broken.

### A day in the life

Dana Kowalczyk sets two alarms on the first Tuesday of the month. The first one is for work and the second one is for the scale.

She steps on it barefoot at 5:10. The display says 171.4 and then SYNCED. Checkpoint is in nineteen days. She needs 168.

At the Groveport cross-dock she clocks in, and Marisol from the clinic trailer waves her over for the monthly shot. Her plan pays all of it but forty dollars, as long as nothing in the app turns red. Marisol swabs the belly and the bruise from last month is already yellow. 'You're tracking,' she says, reading the tablet.

At lunch a new loader, Trey, eats a whole sub. Somebody asks him, friendly, whether he's between courses. He says he isn't on anything. The table goes quiet while everyone waits for him to explain.

After shift she picks up Eli, who is ten and wants to know if he can get the shot like Jaden in Kentucky. 'Not in Ohio, bud.' He says Jaden's mom drives him across the bridge. Dana says Jaden's mom has a different car.

At home Rob is in the garage with the vial he gets from a guy in Reynoldsburg. His coverage dropped in March when he missed 5 percent by a pound and a half. He draws it into an insulin syringe and squints at the lines.

After dinner, which is chicken and half of everything else, they sit on the couch. Rob says the evening is nice. Dana agrees. Neither of them can remember what they used to talk about.

### Safety ledger

- **Gastrointestinal (nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation, reflux)** (established): Still the leading cause of discontinuation on full-dose regimens and the leading cause of conditional-track checkpoint failure. - **Gallbladder disease** (established): Elevated with rapid weight loss; absolute cases up with volume; no change in per-user risk. *Response:* Labelled.
- **Pancreatitis** (suspected): Still no causal confirmation of acute pancreatitis; absolute risk low.
